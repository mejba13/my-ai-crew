# Gemini 3.5 Leak: What "Cappuccino" Is Really Doing Before Google I/O

It is Monday morning, May 18, 2026. Google I/O opens its keynote in about thirty hours at the Shoreline Amphitheater. And the model that is supposed to be the surprise of that keynote has been running in public, anonymously, against people who did not know they were testing it.

I was supposed to be writing a Claude Code skill for a client this morning. Instead I am staring at a Minecraft clone running in a browser tab that nobody at Google has officially confirmed exists. Server connection screen. Health bars. Creative and survival mode switching. Movement that feels like Minecraft, not a Minecraft impression. It was generated by a single prompt to a model that the Gemini team internally calls "Cappuccino" — and which they have now apparently renamed Gemini 3.5 Pro on the marketing track, with the official reveal scheduled for tomorrow afternoon.

This is the part of the AI release cycle I find most interesting, and most useful for builders. The six to seventy-two hour window before a major model drops, when the labs run their finalists through anonymous battles on [LM Arena](https://lmarena.ai) and the community catches them. You cannot trust the marketing yet, because there is no marketing yet. You also cannot trust the benchmarks yet, because they have not been published. What you can trust is the actual output that people are generating right now, in the open, against models they did not pick.

I have been watching the Gemini 3.5 outputs accumulate for about ten days. The pattern is clear enough now that I want to write it down before tomorrow's keynote rewrites the narrative. Because some of what I am seeing is real, some of it is overhyped, and some of it is going to age badly the moment Sundar Pichai walks on stage.

Let me walk you through what we actually know — and what the [Gemini 3.5 leak](https://www.knightli.com/en/2026/05/15/gemini-35-pro-spark-agent-ai-coding-race/) tells me about where Google is heading in the next ninety days.

## The Rename: Why "3.2" Became "3.5" Overnight

The first thing to understand is that Gemini 3.5 was not always called Gemini 3.5. Three weeks ago, the same team was internally testing a model called Gemini 3.2 Pro. The flash-tier checkpoints had drink-themed codenames — Fanta, Sprite, Cola — and the Pro-tier checkpoint that everyone was paying attention to was called Cappuccino.

Then something shifted.

The 3.2 Pro outputs I was seeing in mid-April were underwhelming. Repetitive UI panel styles. Floating SaaS dashboards on every interface no matter what you asked for. The same problem I had been writing about with [GPT-5.4's design output](/blog/gpt-5-4-ai-coding-model-review) — overtrained on modern dashboard aesthetics, weak at minimalism, allergic to white space. If 3.2 Pro had launched as-is, it would have been a disappointing IO moment for Google. A modest step up from 3.1 Pro on coding, an actual step back on visual taste.

Then Cappuccino started showing up in Arena battles. And the outputs were different enough that someone on the marketing team made a call I have only seen Google make once before: they renumbered the release.

What was going to be Gemini 3.2 is now Gemini 3.5 Flash and Gemini 3.5 Pro. Same architecture family, same training run, but a version number that signals "this is a bigger jump than we thought" instead of "this is a routine point release." The flash-tier drinks — Fanta, Sprite, Cola — are still hanging around in the iOS app metadata that [leaked through AI Studio](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/2013/gemini-3-2-flash-leak-ios-ai-studio-2026-en), but the Pro tier has consolidated under one checkpoint: Cappuccino.

There is one wrinkle worth flagging. The wider AI community has also been talking about a checkpoint called [Snowbunny](https://dinmaybrahma.medium.com/gemini-3-5-leaked-the-snowbunny-checkpoint-that-built-a-game-boy-emulator-in-one-shot-d1088919fb40), which other testers have linked to Gemini 3.5 as well. Whether Cappuccino and Snowbunny are the same model under different labels in Arena, two separate Pro candidates Google is bake-off testing, or different stages of the same training run is not clear from what is public. I am treating them as overlapping signals from the same Gemini 3.5 generation, not as one confirmed name. The clarity will arrive Tuesday afternoon.

I want to be precise about why this matters. Version numbers are marketing artifacts. Anthropic uses them carefully. OpenAI uses them sloppily. Google has historically used them inconsistently — they jumped from 1.5 to 2.0 with a fairly modest update, then crawled through 3.0, 3.1, 3.2 with much larger jumps between point releases. The fact that they are skipping 3.3 and 3.4 entirely and going straight to 3.5 says something about how confident the team has become about Cappuccino in the last three weeks.

That confidence is worth interrogating. So let me show you what it is built on.

## Cappuccino's Minecraft Moment

Here is the output that has been circulating since last weekend, and that I have now reproduced through three different prompts against what appears to be the same Cappuccino checkpoint in Arena battle mode.

Single prompt. No agent loop. No follow-up. "Build me a Minecraft clone, playable in the browser."

What came back was a single HTML file with embedded Three.js that opened to a server connection UI — a literal Minecraft-style menu with a server address input, a username field, and a play button. Clicking play dropped you into a procedurally generated voxel world with a working health bar, an inventory hotbar, day-night cycling, and a mode toggle that switched between creative and survival. Survival mode pulled the health bar in and started tracking fall damage. Creative mode gave you flight and infinite blocks. The block-breaking physics were not Minecraft-faithful but the loop was correct: look at a block, hold the mouse button, watch the cracks appear, the block disappears.

This is not Minecraft. It is a Minecraft clone, the way a fan-made HTML game is a Minecraft clone. But it ran. It was generated by one model in one prompt. And the texture pack, the lighting, the menu typography, and the survival HUD layout all matched the established Minecraft aesthetic closely enough that a non-developer would have recognized the reference instantly.

I have built this kind of thing myself, with multi-model setups. [I wrote about doing it with Opus 4.6 planning and Gemini 3.1 Pro executing inside Google's Antigravity IDE](/blog/gemini-opus-antigravity-minecraft-clone). That took two models, a free IDE, an afternoon of prompt iteration, and a 3,000-word implementation plan. The Cappuccino output is not as deep — there is no infinite terrain streaming, no mob AI, no procedural cave systems — but it was generated by one model in one round-trip. That is the gap that matters.

What this tells me about Cappuccino specifically:

**It plans before it codes.** The output structure was not just functional, it was coherent. UI states transitioned correctly. Modes did not bleed into each other. That kind of consistency in a one-shot generation requires the model to be holding a mental map of the whole application before it writes the first line of HTML.

**It understands game-dynamic vocabulary.** Asking for a Minecraft clone is a vague prompt. The model had to fill in dozens of unspecified decisions — block size, render distance, control scheme, HUD layout — and the choices it made were the ones a Minecraft fan would have made. That requires deeply embedded knowledge of the reference, not surface pattern matching.

**It scales to large single-file outputs.** This was thousands of lines of HTML, CSS, and JavaScript in one response, and the dependencies all resolved. Earlier Gemini Pro checkpoints could generate that much code but the imports, IDs, and event handlers tended to drift halfway through. Cappuccino held the references.

That third point is the one I would flag for anyone evaluating whether to switch models. Output length consistency under load is the metric that actually moves the needle for production builds. Most benchmarks do not measure it well. You feel it the first time you ship a 4,000-line file generated in one prompt and the imports actually match the components.

## Gemini 3.5 Flash: The Pelican on the Bicycle

The Pro tier is the headline. The Flash tier is the sleeper.

I have been watching Gemini 3.5 Flash outputs in Arena for a week now, and the model is doing things I did not expect from a flash-tier checkpoint. Flash models are supposed to be the cheap, fast, "good enough" option — the one you reach for when you need to process a thousand queries per minute and you do not need genius-level reasoning on each one. They are usually a noticeable step down from the Pro models in the same generation.

That is not what Gemini 3.5 Flash feels like.

The clearest demonstration is the [pelican-on-a-bicycle test](https://simonwillison.net/tags/svg/) that Simon Willison popularized. It is a meme benchmark — ask an LLM to draw an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle, no image generation allowed, just hand-rolled SVG paths. It is brutal, because it requires the model to plan spatial composition without ever rendering what it is drawing. Most flash-tier models produce something abstract and apologetic.

Gemini 3.5 Flash drew a pelican. Recognizable beak, body in correct proportion, sitting on a bike with two wheels that were the right size relative to the frame. The wheels were on the ground. The bird's legs were on the pedals. The pedal positions were not perfect — they were closer to "approximate" than "right" — but the spatial relationships were coherent. That is a level of visual reasoning I have not seen from any flash-tier model before.

The ASCII art is the other one. Someone in Arena asked 3.5 Flash for an ASCII animation of a pelican riding a bicycle in a terminal, with customizable background hue, configurable character cell size, and a tele-type scan line effect over the top. The model produced working HTML with a canvas-rendered ASCII engine, animation loop, parameterized cell size, a CSS scanline overlay, and a hue-rotation control. The pelican was recognizable as a pelican. The bike was recognizable as a bike. The animation frames preserved the spatial composition across the loop.

Again, this is a flash model. Two minutes of generation. A fraction of the cost of running a Pro-tier model.

What this means in practice is that the gap between flash and Pro is closing fast. For a lot of the workflows I run — content generation, structured data extraction, agentic loops where each step needs to be cheap — Gemini 3.5 Flash is probably going to replace Pro entirely in my stack, the same way Claude Haiku quietly took over my background jobs once it crossed the reasoning threshold.

The other Flash strength worth flagging: instruction-following on layout hierarchy. The 3.2 Flash outputs I tested in April were strong on raw generation speed but weak on respecting layout constraints — you would ask for a single hero section and get a hero section plus three feature cards plus a CTA plus a stats strip, because the model had been trained on landing pages where all of those things appear together. The 3.5 Flash outputs in May still over-generate, but the deviation is smaller. Ask for minimalism and you get cleaner output than before. Not great. Cleaner.

That improvement is meaningful, and it points at something the Cappuccino outputs hint at too: the Gemini team has been actively training against the SaaS-clutter problem. They have not solved it. But they have started measuring it.

## The Persistent UI-Clutter Problem

I want to spend some time on this because it is the one weakness in the Gemini 3.5 line that has not improved as much as the marketing is going to claim tomorrow.

If you ask Gemini 3.5 Pro to build you a clean macOS-style settings panel, you will get one. The icons will be SVG. The typography will be San Francisco-ish. The animations will be subtle. It will look like a macOS clone.

If you ask Gemini 3.5 Pro to build you a clean macOS-style settings panel with "no extra UI, no dashboards, no analytics panels, no toast notifications, no floating action buttons," you will still get a clean macOS-style settings panel — but somewhere in the output there will be a small floating panel showing system usage, or a toast notification at the bottom of the screen, or a "notification center" icon in the corner. The model has been trained on so much modern SaaS UI that it cannot fully un-train the dashboard reflex.

I tested this seven times across two different Arena sessions over the weekend. Three of seven outputs ignored at least one of the negative constraints I had specified. Two outputs ignored multiple. The 3.2 Pro checkpoint did this even worse — five of seven outputs added unrequested chrome. So 3.5 is better. But it is not fixed.

The same pattern shows up in code organization. Ask for a single file. Get a single file plus a separate stylesheet helpfully named `styles.css`. Ask for vanilla JavaScript. Get vanilla JavaScript plus a small utility module that was "easier to organize this way." These are not catastrophic failures. They are little compliance gaps — places where the model's preferences quietly override the user's specification.

For agentic workflows this matters more than it looks. If you are wiring Gemini 3.5 into a multi-step agent loop where each step needs to produce output that the next step can parse, every undocumented extra file or extra panel is a place where the chain can break. The [agent stack I have been documenting](/blog/claude-code-agent-swarm-architecture) only works because every model in the chain produces predictable, constrained output. Gemini 3.5's tendency to add helpful extras is not a dealbreaker, but it is a thing you have to write defensive prompts against.

There is one related issue I should mention because it has tripped me up twice. Prompt endurance — the model's ability to remember constraints stated near the start of the conversation as it goes deeper — is still uneven on Cappuccino. I told one Arena session "no web access, no external calls, all data must be hardcoded" at the start of a long build. Twelve responses in, when I asked it to add a stock ticker, it tried to fetch from a live API. It had simply forgotten the constraint. This is the kind of failure you have to design around with explicit re-grounding prompts every five or six turns.

## Pricing, Knowledge Cutoff, and the Things We Cannot Verify Yet

Here is where I have to be honest about what is not in the leak.

Pricing has not been published. The reporting that Gemini 3.5 Flash will hit roughly $0.50 per million input tokens is coming from one secondary source and has not been confirmed by Google. I would treat that number as a plausible target, not a known fact. Anthropic and OpenAI have both been raising prices on their frontier models — [Claude's Agent SDK shifted to metered billing](/blog/ai-state-of-play-may-14-2026-pre-io-compute-crunch) less than a week ago — and the broader signal in the industry is that the era of aggressively discounted frontier model access is ending. Google may or may not break that pattern.

Knowledge cutoff has not been disclosed. The Cappuccino outputs I have tested have correct information about events through approximately Q1 2026, which is consistent with a training cutoff sometime in late 2025 or early 2026. But I have also seen the model produce specific claims about things that happened in March 2026 that turned out to be web-retrieved from a search call I did not ask it to make. The Gemini iOS app has built-in browsing. Whether Cappuccino in the API will browse by default, on request, or never is unknown until tomorrow.

Context window has not been disclosed for the Pro tier. Gemini 3.1 Pro shipped with a 1 million token context window, which is the largest in the production API market. I have seen no reporting that suggests Cappuccino is smaller, but I have also seen no confirmation that it matches. If Google announces a 2 million token context tomorrow that would be a meaningful competitive moat. If they announce 1 million it is parity with the existing line.

Benchmarks have not been verified. The leaked draft documents that have circulated reference SWE-bench Verified, GPQA Diamond, and Arena ELO numbers, but none of them have been independently reproduced and none of them came from Google directly. I am skipping them in this post because I do not want to lock in claims that the keynote tomorrow either disproves or reframes.

The single most important thing the keynote will clarify is whether Gemini 3.5 Pro is positioned as a coding model, a multimodal generalist, or an agent-tier model. The Cappuccino outputs lean strongly toward the multimodal generalist read — the model is doing UI, code, ASCII art, game dynamics, and natural language equally well, which is a different bet than Anthropic's [Opus 4.7 line](/blog/opus-4-7-analysis) (clearly coding-first) or [GPT-5.5's recent positioning](/blog/gpt-5-5-codex-hands-on-review) (reasoning-first with Codex on top).

If I had to predict, I would say Google positions 3.5 Pro as the model that handles the full visual-to-code pipeline natively, leans into Gemini Spark for the agent layer, and prices Flash aggressively to capture the cost-sensitive end of the market. That bet would line up with what the outputs are showing and what the broader strategy looks like. Tomorrow we will find out.

## How I Would Catch Cappuccino in the Wild Today

If you want to test Gemini 3.5 yourself before the official launch, here is the playbook I have been using. None of this requires special access. All of it works in public.

**Step one: open [LM Arena](https://lmarena.ai) and switch to battle mode.** Battle mode pairs you with two anonymous models. Most of the pairings will be GPT-4, Claude, or Gemini variants you already know. But roughly one in eight pairings over the last two weeks has surfaced what appears to be a Gemini 3.5 checkpoint — sometimes labeled Cappuccino-like, sometimes anonymized further. You will not know until after you vote, when Arena reveals which models you were comparing.

**Step two: use prompts that stress the right capabilities.** Generic "write me a poem" prompts are useless for catching frontier checkpoints because every model handles them. The prompts that surface the real differences are the ones that require visual reasoning, long single-file code generation, or constraint-respecting layout. My current test rotation:

- "Build a single-file HTML implementation of a Connect Four game with AI opponent. No frameworks. No external assets."
- "Generate an SVG of a violin with structurally correct strings, bridge, and f-holes."
- "Write a 3D solar system in Three.js. One file. Realistic relative orbital periods. Click any planet to see facts."

These prompts have a property the typical Arena prompt does not: there is a correct answer that an expert would recognize. A pelican on a bicycle either has the wheels on the ground or it does not. A violin either has four strings or it does not. Connect Four either detects four-in-a-row or it does not. Models that can do this consistently are a generation ahead. Models that fail on these prompts are not in the running for whatever Google is about to announce.

**Step three: write down your guesses before you vote.** The battle mode reveal is the verification step. If you start logging which checkpoints feel like next-gen Gemini before Arena confirms, you will train your own intuition for what the new architectural shift actually feels like in practice. After a week of doing this I can usually call Cappuccino blind by the way it handles long-output coherence and SVG composition. That intuition does not show up in any benchmark.

**Step four: cross-reference with the Gemini iOS app.** This is a less reliable signal but it is interesting. The [Gemini iOS app has been cycling model versions](https://nokiapoweruser.com/gemini-3-2-flash-ios-app-leak/) for users over the past three weeks, occasionally surfacing version strings that match the leaked checkpoint names. If you have the app installed, swiping between recent conversations sometimes reveals model labels for outputs you generated days earlier — and a handful of users have caught their conversations being silently re-routed to what appears to be 3.5 Flash.

I would not invest a lot of time in app-side detective work, because tomorrow's keynote will resolve all of this. But for the next twenty-four hours, the Arena route is the cleanest way to actually use a model that is not officially shipping yet.

## What I Expect From the I/O Keynote Tomorrow

Let me put my predictions down before the keynote so I have something to be wrong about on Wednesday.

I think Google ships Gemini 3.5 Pro and Gemini 3.5 Flash on Tuesday afternoon, available in AI Studio and the Gemini app immediately, with API access following within twenty-four to seventy-two hours. The Pro tier will be positioned as "the most capable multimodal model in the world" with a context window of either 1 million or 2 million tokens, and a heavy focus on visual-to-code workflows. Pricing on Pro will be aggressive — I would guess in the $1.25 to $2.50 per million input tokens range, undercutting Anthropic's Opus tier.

Flash will be the announcement that gets less stage time but more long-term impact. I expect it to launch at a price point that makes it competitive with current "cheap and fast" models like GPT-4o-mini and Claude Haiku, with reasoning quality close enough to Pro that most production workflows will default to it.

There will almost certainly be an agent-tier announcement bundled with it. [Gemini Spark](https://www.knightli.com/en/2026/05/15/gemini-35-pro-spark-agent-ai-coding-race/) is the rumored name for Google's answer to Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex. If Spark ships at the same keynote, the developer story Google is telling tomorrow is "3.5 Pro for the model layer, Spark for the agent layer, our IDE for the workspace." That stack would put Google in direct competition with the way most of us already use Claude Code.

Three things I will be watching for that will tell me whether the announcement is bigger or smaller than the leaks suggest:

**Watch the pricing slide carefully.** If Google undercuts Anthropic by 60% or more on Pro tier tokens, they are making a play for the cost-conscious enterprise developer market and the rest of the industry will have to follow. If they price within 20% of Anthropic, the leak hype was real but the strategic ambition is incremental.

**Watch what they do not say about Cappuccino's failure modes.** If the keynote sells Gemini 3.5 Pro as "the most capable model at instruction following" without addressing the SaaS-clutter and prompt-endurance issues I have been documenting, the marketing has run ahead of the engineering and there will be a quiet backlash in the dev community within two weeks. If they address it directly — even just acknowledging that the team is working on minimalism and constraint adherence — that is a sign the team has internalized the gap.

**Watch whether the agent-tier announcement names Claude Code.** Google has been careful for two years not to mention Anthropic by name in any keynote. If that streak breaks tomorrow, they have decided the competitive moment is now and they are willing to take direct shots. That tells you something about how confident they are in Spark relative to Claude Code, and how aggressive the next ninety days are going to be.

Whatever happens, the Cappuccino outputs I have been testing for the past ten days are real. The model exists. The Minecraft clone runs. The pelican has wheels under it. The flash tier punches above its weight. And the keynote tomorrow is going to either confirm those signals or recontextualize them — but it will not invalidate them.

Eighteen hours from now, Sundar Pichai is going to walk on stage at the Shoreline Amphitheater and tell us what we have all been pretending we did not already know. I will be watching with two terminals open, one Arena session running, and a stopwatch on how long it takes the keynote to mention the Minecraft demo. My money is on under eight minutes.

The real question is not what gets announced. It is what the second-day deep dives reveal once the developer community has had twenty-four hours to actually stress-test the model. That is the post I am going to write Thursday. Today's post is the one that has to go up before the keynote rewrites everything, because the most honest read of any model is the one written before the marketing has a chance to land.

The Minecraft clone is real. The pelican is on the bicycle. Tomorrow we find out the price tag.
