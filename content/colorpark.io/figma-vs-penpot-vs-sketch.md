# Figma vs Penpot vs Sketch: The Honest 2026 Verdict

A senior product designer at a fintech in Berlin opened her laptop on a Tuesday morning in March and did something her CTO had been quietly hoping she'd do for two years.

She exported every active project from Figma and started rebuilding the team's design system in Penpot.

Not because Figma broke. Figma works. Figma works incredibly well, and the team had paid for it without complaint since 2021. She did it because the company's compliance team had spent four months trying to get a satisfactory answer about where Figma's servers stored personally identifiable customer data, and the design team's $45-per-editor Organization seats — eleven of them, every month — were starting to feel less like a tool budget and more like a tax on staying inside someone else's house.

That decision is happening in design teams everywhere right now.

It's also not the right decision for most of them.

The choice between figma vs penpot vs sketch in 2026 isn't a feature list. It's a question about who you are, what you're building, and which compromises you're willing to make on price, ownership, and craft. Three tools. Three completely different philosophies. Three different bets on where design is heading.

Most articles about this comparison will hand you a checklist with green ticks and red Xs. This one won't. Because the green ticks lie. They tell you Penpot has components and Figma has components and treat that like equivalent information, when the actual experience of using each is so different that the word "components" is doing dangerous lifting.

Here's what's actually true about each of these tools right now — pricing verified, tradeoffs sharpened, and the verdict no one else is willing to write plainly.

---

## The Verdict, Up Front

Figma is the design tool everyone is currently stuck with — for now. It's the safest professional choice in 2026, and it's the most expensive one, and both of those things will keep being true until something dislodges it.

Penpot is the design tool you'd build if you started over. It's open-source, browser-native, deployable on your own infrastructure, and it ships actual CSS Grid and Flexbox instead of "auto-layout" — which means designers and developers are finally speaking the same language at the layer where it matters. It's also rougher around the edges than Figma. Both of those statements are true at the same time.

Sketch is the design tool you remember loving. It's still excellent. It's still Mac-only. It's still the fastest, most native, most beautifully built design app on any operating system. It's also a tool with a smaller and quieter community than it had in 2018, optimized for a kind of designer that fewer companies are hiring.

That's the verdict. Now here's why each of those statements is true, what it costs to ignore them, and which of these tools actually deserves your team's next quarter.

## Three Tools. Three Philosophies. Three Bets.

Before pricing tables and feature checklists, you need the worldview of each tool. Tools are arguments. They're built by people who believe specific things about how design should work, and those beliefs leak into every menu, every keyboard shortcut, every default. If you don't understand the argument, you can't tell when the tool is fighting you.

**Figma believes design is a multiplayer document.** Everything else flows from that. The browser-first architecture. The shareable link as primary unit of distribution. The PMs and engineers who comment in the same canvas as the designer. The component libraries that update across thirty files at once. Figma's bet — and it's been the right bet for a decade — is that the bottleneck in product design is collaboration friction, and that whoever removes the most friction wins.

**Penpot believes design is infrastructure.** Files belong to you. Layout primitives are CSS, because CSS is what ships. Tokens are first-class, because tokens are how teams scale. The whole tool is licensed under MPL 2.0 and runnable on your own servers, because lock-in is a tax that compounds. Penpot's bet is that as design matures, teams will care more about ownership, portability, and developer parity than they care about the tool with the prettiest landing page.

**Sketch believes design is craft.** It's a Mac app, and it's unapologetic about that. It loads instantly. It runs offline. It respects the operating system. The web app exists, but it exists to serve the native experience — not replace it. Sketch's bet is that there will always be designers for whom the difference between a beautifully native tool and a competent web app is the difference between flow state and forced labor, and those designers will pay for the better experience.

Three bets. All three of them have been at least partly correct. The question for your team in 2026 is which bet is most correct *for the work you actually do*.

Let's walk through each tool with that lens.

## Figma: The Default, the Sprawl, and the Lock-In

Figma is what you choose when you don't want to fight your team about it. Verified pricing as of May 2026: Starter is free for up to 3 design files and 3 FigJam boards. Professional is $15 per editor per month on annual billing ($18 monthly), Organization is $45 per editor per month, and Enterprise sits at $75 per editor per month. Dev Mode adds another $25 per developer per month on Professional, $35 on Enterprise. FigJam Collab is $3 per collaborator per month if you want lighter seats for non-designers. Sources: figma.com/pricing and the Figma help center's billing updates.

That's a lot of pricing surface area, and it's deliberate. Figma post-IPO is a public company that needs to grow revenue per customer, and the answer it landed on was: ship more products, charge for more seats. At Config 2025, Figma shipped four new products in a single keynote — Draw (a vector playground), Sites (publish frames as live websites), Buzz (brand-safe content for marketing teams), and Make (an AI prompt-to-prototype tool that generates React/Tailwind code). On top of First Draft for AI-assisted UI generation, Slides for presentations, and the existing FigJam whiteboard.

The official line is that 76% of Figma customers now use two or more Figma products. The unofficial translation: your renewal conversation next year will probably include at least one extra product line item.

### What Figma Does Brilliantly

Real-time collaboration with no caveats. Open a file, share a link, ten people can work in it at once and the cursors move smoothly. Nothing else in this comparison matches this experience. Penpot has real-time multiplayer too, but Figma's rendering and sync layer is more battle-tested under load.

Plugin ecosystem with over 5,000 community plugins, ranging from icon libraries to AI-powered design assistants to design system tooling. If a workflow gap exists, somebody has shipped a plugin for it.

Dev Mode is the cleanest design-to-developer handoff in the industry. Designers can mark items as ready for dev, annotate measurements and intent, and developers can pull component code, CSS, and (with Code Connect) actual snippets from the team's component library. Dev Mode runs inside VS Code, and the MCP server lets AI coding agents like Claude Code pull design context directly. For teams that already operate this way, see [the full design-to-code workflow breakdown](https://www.colorpark.io/designers-ship-code-ai) — the integration story between Figma and AI IDEs is the strongest reason to stay.

The plugin and Dev Mode advantages compound. A team that has invested two years in custom Figma plugins and Code Connect mappings is not casually migrating to anything.

### What Figma Does Badly

Offline. You can sort of cache files. You cannot reliably edit them on a plane. For designers who travel, work from cafes with bad WiFi, or just lose their connection for an hour, Figma is consistently the worst experience in this comparison. Sketch does this in its sleep. Penpot at least lets you self-host on a local network. Figma assumes your internet is always fine.

Performance with large files. Once a Figma file crosses a few hundred frames or carries dense vector content, the browser tab gets sluggish. There are workarounds — splitting files, separate libraries, archiving old projects — but they're workarounds. The architecture has limits.

Pricing transparency. The pricing page is not a price. The pricing page is a starting point for a conversation that ends with a Dev Mode add-on, an Organization seat upgrade, a few FigJam Collab seats, and a Slides license. Teams routinely report that their actual annual Figma spend is 50–80% higher than what the marketing site implies.

### The Hidden Cost: Lock-In

Figma files are stored in Figma's proprietary binary format. They live on Figma's servers. The export options are PNG, SVG, PDF, and a Figma file that only Figma can open. Penpot can import some Figma files, but the round trip is lossy and components don't always survive.

This was an abstract problem until December 18, 2023, when Adobe and Figma mutually terminated their proposed $20 billion merger after the European Commission and the UK Competition and Markets Authority signaled they would not approve it. Adobe paid Figma a $1 billion break-up fee. Figma went public in July 2025 and shares surged up to 275% on debut, briefly pushing its market cap near $68 billion.

Figma is not getting acquired. Figma is now a public company with shareholders, quarterly earnings pressure, and a track record of raising prices. The lock-in question used to be theoretical. It isn't anymore.

### Who Should NOT Pick Figma

Solo designers and freelancers paying out of pocket. The price-to-value ratio gets ugly fast for one-person teams.

Organizations in regulated industries — finance, healthcare, government — that need full data sovereignty and can't get satisfactory answers about where files are stored and who can subpoena them.

Teams that genuinely work offline. Plane time, train time, conference time, traveling-to-a-client time. If that's more than 10% of your week, you'll resent Figma.

Everyone else who wants the team's design tool to be the least-friction part of their week, picking Figma is still defensible. It's just no longer obvious.

## Penpot: The Open-Source Bet That Finally Grew Up

The version of Penpot that designers wrote off in 2022 is not the Penpot that exists in 2026. That's the most important sentence to internalize before you evaluate it.

Penpot 2.0 shipped in 2024 with a complete UI redesign, a new components system, and the first design tool to ship native CSS Grid Layout alongside Flex Layout. The 2.x line that followed added native design tokens, refined real-time collaboration, and improved performance significantly. In May 2026, Penpot is a credible production tool for UI work. With caveats.

### Penpot Pricing, Verified

Penpot's hosted SaaS has a free Professional plan that includes unlimited teams, no file limits, and full design and collaboration features. The Unlimited plan is $7 per user per month, capped at $175 per month total — meaning a team of 50 designers pays the same as a team of 25 once you hit the cap. The Enterprise plan is a flat $950 per month regardless of headcount.

For self-hosting, Penpot is licensed under MPL 2.0 and free. You run it on your own infrastructure — Docker, Kubernetes, a VPS, your existing server. Managed self-hosting through services like Elestio starts at $14 per month for backups, SSL, and updates. Sources: penpot.app/pricing and elest.io/open-source/penpot.

Compare that to Figma's $45-per-editor Organization plan. A team of 25 designers on Figma Organization pays $13,500 per year — minimum, before Dev Mode seats. The same team on Penpot Unlimited pays $2,100 per year, capped. On self-hosted Penpot, they pay the cost of a small Linux box.

This is not a small difference. This is the kind of difference that gets brought up in CFO meetings.

### What Penpot Does Brilliantly

CSS Grid and Flex Layout as native primitives. This is the feature that should make every developer on your team pay attention. When a Penpot designer builds a layout, it isn't a designer's abstraction of a layout — it's the actual CSS the developer will write. Click into a frame in Penpot and you see grid-template-columns, gap values, and align-items as first-class controls. The translation step between design and code, the one that has bled hours from product teams for fifteen years, gets thinner.

Native design tokens. Not as a plugin. Not as a managed library you set up after onboarding. Tokens are baked into the tool. Token sets, themes, the ability to enable or disable combinations, propagation when a value changes — all of it is in the box. This is not how Figma handles tokens, where the standard answer is "install Tokens Studio and configure it for your team." Penpot treats tokens as infrastructure, which is what they are.

Open file format. Penpot files are SVG-based. They live in standard web formats. If Penpot vanished tomorrow — which it won't, but imagine — your files would still open. Your tokens would still parse. Your component definitions would still be readable. Compare that to a Figma binary that requires Figma to render.

Self-hosting. For agencies under NDA, financial services teams, healthcare orgs handling protected data, and any team that has spent a procurement cycle answering the question "where do our design files actually live?" — Penpot is the only answer in this comparison that lets you say "on our infrastructure, behind our firewall, under our control."

### What Penpot Does Badly

Plugin ecosystem. Penpot has plugins. It does not have 5,000 plugins. The community is growing, the plugin API is real, but the ecosystem depth that Figma users take for granted does not exist yet.

Performance on large files. Penpot runs in the browser like Figma, and its rendering engine is less optimized. Designers report noticeable slowdown on files with 100+ frames or heavy vector content. For most product design work this is fine. For massive design systems and dense documentation files, it's an active friction point.

Dev Mode equivalence. Developers can inspect elements and copy CSS. There is no Dev Mode equivalent yet — no annotated handoff feed, no framework-specific code snippets at the depth Figma offers, no Code Connect-style mapping to your component library. Penpot's open file format and CSS-native layouts narrow the gap, but the experience isn't matched feature-for-feature.

Onboarding for teams trained on Figma. Most modern designers learned product design in Figma. Switching them costs three to six weeks of muscle memory rebuild — which is a real cost even when the tool is free.

### The Hidden Win: Sovereignty

The real reason to consider Penpot in 2026 isn't the price. It's the answer it gives you to questions you might not have realized you were going to be asked.

Where does this design file live? On our servers.

Who can access it? Only people we've authorized.

What happens if Penpot raises prices? We don't care.

What happens if Penpot gets acquired? We have the source code.

What happens if a regulator asks us about our design data? We hand them an architecture diagram.

For a small team this is mostly philosophical. For an enterprise design org with compliance obligations and a procurement department, this is the entire game.

### Who Should NOT Pick Penpot

Teams that depend on a specific Figma plugin or workflow that doesn't have a Penpot equivalent. List your top ten plugins. Check each one. If three of them are mission-critical and missing, Penpot will frustrate you.

Teams whose engineering organization has built deep Figma Dev Mode integrations or Code Connect mappings. Throwing that away to save money on seats is not a good trade.

Designers who work primarily in dense, performance-heavy files — large illustration work, dense documentation, multi-thousand-frame design systems. The rendering engine isn't there yet.

Solo freelancers who need to onboard new clients fast and every client expects Figma. The path of least resistance is the one your clients are already on.

For everyone else — and especially for teams who care about ownership, agencies tired of $45 seats, organizations in regulated industries, and developer-led product teams who think in CSS — Penpot has crossed the threshold from "interesting" to "actually evaluate this."

## Sketch: Still the Best Mac App Nobody Talks About Anymore

Sketch is in a strange position in 2026. It's still excellent. It's still beloved by the designers who use it. It's also, in market share terms, the smallest of the three tools in this comparison, and the one whose community discourse has gotten quieter year over year.

That's worth examining, because the reasons people leave Sketch and the reasons Sketch is still worth picking are mostly the same reasons.

### Sketch Pricing, Verified

The Standard plan is $12 per editor per month on annual billing, $14 monthly. The Business plan is $22 per editor per month on annual. There's also a Mac-only license for $120 as a one-time payment, which gets you the native app and one year of updates — a model that simply doesn't exist at Figma or Penpot. Source: sketch.com/pricing.

That one-time license is a quietly important detail. For a designer who wants to own their tool and never think about a subscription again, Sketch is the only option in this comparison that actually offers that.

### What Sketch Does Brilliantly

Native macOS performance. This is the headline feature. Sketch is a Mac app, in the way that designers from 2015 remember Mac apps being. It launches instantly. Files open instantly. Pan and zoom feel direct, not laggy. There is no browser tab between you and your work. For designers whose flow state depends on tool responsiveness, the difference between Sketch and a browser-based tool is enormous and completely undersold by feature comparisons.

Plugin ecosystem. Sketch has over 1,000 plugins built by a community that has been making them for nearly a decade. Animation tools, accessibility checkers, content generators, design system utilities — the depth here is genuinely strong, and the quality bar is high.

Offline-first reality. Sketch was built before "cloud-first" was the default. You can work entirely offline, save files locally, sync later. For travel, for spotty connections, for designers who live somewhere with unreliable internet, Sketch is the only real option.

Stability and craft. Sketch the company has been at this since 2010. The app is mature, well-tested, and updates rarely break things. For designers who hate the feeling of their tool changing under them every quarter, Sketch is calm in a way Figma is not.

### What Sketch Does Badly

Cross-platform. Sketch is Mac-only. Period. If anyone on your team uses Windows or Linux — including engineers, PMs, or external collaborators — Sketch is a non-starter for that workflow. The web app exists for previews and feedback, not for editing.

Real-time collaboration. Sketch has commenting, has a web workspace, has a co-editing feature in the Mac app on annual plans. It does not have Figma-grade real-time multiplayer. For teams whose primary mode is "five people in the same file at once," Sketch will feel slow.

Community gravity. The energy in the design tool community in 2026 is not in Sketch. New plugins ship slower. Tutorials and educational content are heavily Figma-skewed. Designers entering the industry are mostly trained on Figma. None of this means Sketch is bad — it means the network effects are working against it.

### The Hidden Strength: Calm

Sketch's underappreciated quality is that it's a tool that doesn't try to be everything. Figma at this point is a design tool, a whiteboard tool, a slides tool, a website builder, an AI prompt-to-prototype tool, and a vector illustration tool. Penpot is staying focused but expanding. Sketch is a design tool. It is incredibly good at being a design tool and it has not noticeably tried to be anything else for years.

For designers who want a craft tool that respects them, who don't want their software to be a platform, who don't want to think about a roadmap of products they'll be charged for next quarter — Sketch is the option that says "we're going to keep doing this thing, and do it well, and not ask you for more."

That's a valuable thing in 2026. It's just not what most teams are buying.

### Who Should NOT Pick Sketch

Cross-platform teams. If a single Windows user matters to your workflow, Sketch is wrong.

Teams whose primary workflow is real-time collaboration with non-designers. Sketch's collaboration story is fine for designer-to-designer work and weak for designer-to-everyone-else.

Designers who want to stay close to where the community innovates. Most experimental design tooling, AI integrations, and plugin development happens in the Figma ecosystem first.

For Mac-native solo designers, small Mac-first studios that prize craft and performance, and teams who genuinely value not being on the constant feature treadmill — Sketch remains the most respectful tool in this comparison. Just go in clear-eyed about what it isn't trying to be.

## The Decision Framework: Five Archetypes, Five Answers

Let's stop talking about features and start talking about teams. Here are the five archetypes that cover most design organizations in 2026, and the right tool for each.

### The Solo Designer or Indie Founder

You're one person. You're paying out of your own pocket. You need to ship fast, look good, and not light money on fire.

**Pick: Penpot.** The free Professional tier covers everything you need. If you ever need more, the Unlimited plan is $7 per month. Your work stays portable. You're not building lock-in into a future you can't afford. The only reason to choose Figma here is if every client you work with insists on it — which happens, and which is the only legitimate Figma case in this archetype.

If you're macOS-only and craft matters more than collaboration, Sketch's $120 one-time license is the most respectful pricing in this comparison. Buy it, own it, never think about it again.

### The 3–10 Person Product Team

You've got designers, PMs, and engineers in the same files. Real-time collaboration is your daily reality. The team needs to ship features without translation losses.

**Pick: Figma.** This is Figma's home turf and it earned the position fairly. The collaboration depth, Dev Mode integration, and plugin ecosystem are genuinely worth the per-seat cost at this team size. The math gets ugly above 25 people. Below that, the productivity gain pays for the seats.

The exception: if your engineering team is heavily CSS-native, your tokens story is a mess, and your design system is the thing you keep falling behind on — evaluate Penpot seriously. The CSS Grid and native tokens story can be a genuine accelerator at this size.

### The Agency with Mixed Clients

You take on work for ten different companies a year. Some of them have Figma standards. Some have legacy Sketch files. Some don't care.

**Pick: Figma as primary, Penpot as backup, Sketch case-by-case.** Agencies don't get to be opinionated about tools — clients are opinionated. Maintain Figma fluency because most clients are there. Build a Penpot capability because regulated and EU-based clients increasingly ask for it. Keep Sketch installed for the Mac-native craft studios that still send Sketch files. Your skill is fluency across the comparison, not allegiance to one tool.

### The Open-Source-First Org

Your company runs on Linux. Your engineers contribute to public repos. Your CTO has feelings about vendor lock-in. Your data sovereignty story matters.

**Pick: Penpot, self-hosted.** This isn't even close. Penpot is the only design tool in this comparison that respects how you actually want to operate. Deploy it on your infrastructure, run it behind your auth, contribute upstream when you find bugs, and stop paying per-seat fees to a public company whose interests are not aligned with yours.

The tradeoffs — smaller plugin ecosystem, weaker AI features, less polish in some corners — are tradeoffs you've made before in your stack and lived with. They're worth it.

### The Mac-Native Craft Studio

You're a small studio, maybe a one-person shop, maybe a five-person collective. Everyone's on a Mac. Speed and feel matter more than collaboration depth. You'd rather your tool be invisible and fast than feature-complete.

**Pick: Sketch.** This is the archetype Sketch was built for and it still serves it best. The Mac-native performance, offline reliability, and one-time-license option are uniquely suited to studios where craft and ownership matter more than network effects. Add a free Penpot account for the rare client file that lands in an open format, and you're set.

## Three Scenarios, Three Decisions

Specifics make tradeoffs real. Here are three concrete teams and the right call for each.

### Scenario 1: A 14-Person Fintech Design Team in the EU

The team is post-Series-B, growing, and gets quarterly compliance reviews. The legal team has questions about every SaaS vendor. Designers currently work in Figma Organization at $45 per editor per month, totaling $7,560 annually. Engineering uses Dev Mode at $25 per dev seat. Total Figma spend approaches $20,000 per year.

The compliance team wants a clear answer about where design files containing internal product roadmaps live. Figma's answer is "on our infrastructure, with these certifications." That's not wrong. It's also not the answer the compliance team wants to hear about UI mockups that show unreleased features.

**Right call: migrate to self-hosted Penpot, keep Figma for one quarter on a wind-down plan.** Run Penpot on the company's existing AWS infrastructure. Use the migration as a forcing function for the design system rebuild that's been on the backlog for a year. Use Penpot's native CSS Grid and design tokens as the foundation. The team will lose Dev Mode polish, gain six figures back over three years, and answer the compliance question definitively.

### Scenario 2: A Six-Person Product Team at a Series-A Startup

The team is shipping daily. Designers, PMs, and two engineers all hop into Figma files together for ten-minute review sessions. Dev Mode is wired into the engineering team's Cursor and VS Code workflows. The startup is burning down a runway and is on Figma Professional for $90 per month.

**Right call: stay on Figma.** The total cost is rounding error compared to the productivity gain. The collaboration depth is worth it. At this size, time is the scarce resource, not money. Worry about tool optimization at the next funding round, not now.

### Scenario 3: A Two-Person Design Studio in San Francisco

Both designers are on Mac. They've been using Figma for client work because that's what clients expected. They're tired of paying $30 per month for two seats they don't fully use, and they're tired of how the tool feels heavier every quarter.

**Right call: Sketch for internal work, Figma seats only when client work demands it.** Both designers buy Sketch licenses for personal craft and personal projects — fast, native, owned. Keep two Figma Professional seats for client work. Use the Sketch experience as a deliberate counterweight to the feature creep of Figma. Some clients will eventually accept Penpot files; offer that when you can.

The point isn't to pick one tool forever. The point is to know what each tool is for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Penpot actually production-ready in 2026 or is it still mostly hype?

Penpot is production-ready for UI and product design work in 2026. Component systems, CSS Grid and Flex layout, native design tokens, real-time collaboration, and prototyping are all stable and shipping. The honest caveats: the plugin ecosystem is much smaller than Figma's, large files (100+ frames) show rendering slowdown, and there's no full Dev Mode equivalent yet. Small studios, freelancers, and developer-led teams report shipping production work in Penpot today. Large enterprises with deep Figma plugin and Dev Mode investments will feel the rough edges. Start with one project before migrating everything.

### How real is the lock-in risk with Figma after the Adobe deal failed and the IPO?

Real, and quietly increasing. Figma is a public company since July 2025 with shareholders, quarterly earnings pressure, and a pricing structure that has steadily expanded across new product lines (Sites, Make, Buzz, Slides, Dev Mode add-ons). Figma files remain in a proprietary binary format with no first-party migration path. None of this means Figma is going to do something hostile tomorrow — it does mean the leverage in the relationship sits firmly with Figma. Teams that care about long-term portability should at minimum keep an exit plan in their disaster recovery doc.

### Is Sketch still relevant in 2026 or has the design industry left it behind?

Sketch is relevant for a smaller, specific audience and largely irrelevant for everyone else. For Mac-native solo designers and small studios that prize speed, offline reliability, and craft, Sketch is the best tool in this comparison. For collaborative teams with mixed-OS members, Sketch is genuinely behind. The 1,000+ plugin ecosystem is mature, the app is stable and well-built, and the one-time-license option is unique. None of that compensates for the cross-platform and real-time collaboration limits if those are your needs. Don't pick Sketch out of nostalgia; pick it because the tradeoffs match your work.

### How do AI features actually compare across Figma, Penpot, and Sketch right now?

Figma is meaningfully ahead in AI integration. Figma Make ships prompt-to-prototype with React/Tailwind code output, First Draft generates UI components, and the MCP server exposes design context to AI coding agents like Claude Code. Penpot's AI integration is community-driven and lighter — open file formats make it agent-friendly in different ways, but the polished first-party experience isn't there. Sketch has the lightest AI story of the three. If AI-augmented design workflows matter to your team this quarter, Figma is the answer. For a fuller view of the broader AI design tooling space, see [the AI design tools breakdown](https://www.colorpark.io/ai-design-tools-ui-creation).

### Which tool gives developers the cleanest handoff in real production work?

Figma Dev Mode is the most polished handoff experience, with annotated specs, framework-specific code snippets, Code Connect mappings to your component library, and VS Code integration. Penpot offers a different proposition: because layouts are native CSS Grid and Flex, and files are SVG-based, developers often find the handoff more direct even without a dedicated Dev Mode — they're reading layouts they would have written themselves. Sketch's inspector tool is competent but lighter than either alternative. The honest answer: Figma if your devs want guided handoff, Penpot if your devs want CSS-native designs they can read directly, Sketch if handoff is a smaller part of your workflow.

### How well does each tool scale for large design systems?

Figma scales best on the surface — team libraries, component publishing, design system analytics on Organization plans, and a deep ecosystem of design system tooling and plugins. Performance starts to bite on very large files but remains the most battle-tested. Penpot scales credibly with native design tokens, CSS Grid primitives, and self-hosting that lets large enterprises put the system on their own infrastructure; the rendering ceiling is the constraint, not the architecture. Sketch scales reasonably for shared libraries but lacks the cross-team analytics and governance features Figma Organization provides. For systems work specifically, [the deeper read on design system thinking](https://www.colorpark.io/design-wins-ai-software-era) applies regardless of which tool you pick.

## Key Takeaways

- The choice between figma vs penpot vs sketch is not a feature comparison; it is a worldview comparison. Figma believes design is multiplayer. Penpot believes design is infrastructure. Sketch believes design is craft. Pick the worldview that fits your work.

- Figma is the safest professional choice in 2026 and the most expensive one. Both will keep being true. If your team is 3–25 people doing collaborative product work and money is not the bottleneck, Figma is still defensible.

- Penpot has crossed the line from "interesting" to "evaluate seriously" — especially for regulated industries, EU teams, agencies tired of per-seat pricing, and developer-led product teams who think in CSS. Native design tokens and CSS Grid are real advantages, not marketing.

- Sketch remains excellent for Mac-native solo designers and small studios that prize speed and ownership. The community has shrunk, the tool has not. The one-time license is uniquely respectful.

- Lock-in is no longer theoretical with Figma. Public company, shareholders, expanding product lines, proprietary file format. Have an exit plan documented even if you never use it.

- Self-hosting is the differentiator nobody talks about until they need it. If your compliance team has ever asked where your design files live, Penpot is the only answer in this comparison that ends the conversation.

- The right answer for most teams is to be fluent in two of these tools, not loyal to one. Figma for client work. Penpot for owned work. Sketch when craft and Mac-native performance matter most. The future of design tooling looks a lot less monogamous than the past five years.
